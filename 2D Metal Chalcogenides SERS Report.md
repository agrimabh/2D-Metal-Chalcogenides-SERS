## 2D Nanosheet Materials of Metal Chalcogenides as a Potential SERS Active Substrate for Chemical and Biochemical Sensing

## Executive Summary

Surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) has emerged as a cornerstone technique in analytical chemistry, offering molecular fingerprinting with sensitivity extending down to the single-molecule level. While traditional SERS substrates have been dominated by noble metals (Au, Ag) leveraging electromagnetic enhancement, two-dimensional (2D) metal chalcogenides (MCs) are increasingly recognized for their superior chemical enhancement (CE) capabilities. This report evaluates the potential of 2D nanosheets of metal chalcogenides, including tantalum disulfide (TaS₂), molybdenum disulfide (MoS₂), and niobium disulfide (NbS₂), as active SERS substrates for chemical and biochemical sensing.

Synthesized through advanced methods like chemical vapor deposition (CVD), liquid exfoliation, and phase engineering, these materials offer tunable electronic properties and abundant density of states (DOS) near the Fermi level. The enhancement mechanisms in 2D MCs are primarily driven by interfacial charge transfer (CT) and resonance effects, often achieving detection limits comparable to or exceeding noble metal systems. Applications discussed herein span the detection of environmental pollutants, antibiotics, and critical biochemical markers, demonstrating the robustness and versatility of 2D

metal chalcogenide platforms for next-generation sensing technologies.

## 1. Introduction

Raman spectroscopy is an invaluable tool for molecular identification, yet its application in trace detection is historically limited by the extremely low scattering cross-section of most molecules, where only approximately one in ten million incident photons undergo inelastic scattering. Surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) overcomes this limitation by providing Raman signal enhancements reaching million-fold to billion-fold levels. Traditionally, these enhancements are achieved using noble metal nanostructures (e.g., gold and silver) that utilize localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) to create "hotspots" of intense electromagnetic fields.

However, noble-metal substrates face challenges such as high cost, poor reproducibility, limited biocompatibility, and undesirable side reactions due to their high catalytic activity. Consequently, 2D materials have emerged as promising alternatives. Among these, transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) and other metal chalcogenides stand out due to their atomic thickness, large surface-to-volume ratio, and tunable electronic structures. Unlike graphene, which primarily relies on ground-state charge transfer, 2D metal chalcogenides offer a diverse array of phases (semiconducting 2H, metallic 1T or 1T') and defect-rich surfaces that can be tailored to maximize Raman enhancement through chemical mechanisms. This paper explores the state-of-the-art in 2D metal chalcogenide SERS substrates, focusing on their synthesis, enhancement mechanisms, and practical sensing applications.

## 2. Synthesis and Characterization

The preparation of high-quality 2D metal chalcogenide nanosheets is critical for achieving reproducible and high-performance SERS substrates. Several techniques have been developed to control the thickness, phase, and defect density of these materials.

## 2.1 Chemical Vapor Deposition (CVD)

CVD is a primary method for producing large-area, uniform 2D films with controllable thickness. For example, few-layered MoTe₂ films have been successfully grown using CVD for the nanomolar detection of biorelevant molecules. Similarly, monolayer MoS₂ nanosheets are prepared in CVD systems using sulfur and molybdenum sources as precursors, where parameters such as temperature (e.g., 850 °C for the Mo source) and atmosphere (Ar) are precisely tuned. Alkali-assisted CVD has also been employed to produce ultrathin metallic 2D NbS₂ flakes with large domain sizes exceeding 160 µm.


## 2.2 Liquid-Phase Exfoliation and Hydrothermal Methods

Liquid-phase exfoliation (LPE) is a scalable method for producing 2D nanosheets from bulk layered materials. When combined with hydrothermal processing, it allows for the synthesis of complex chalcogenides like few-layer NbTe₂ nanosheets. MoS₂ nanosheets prepared via a simple hydrothermal method can be exfoliated into monolayers through sonication without the need for surfactants. The hydrothermal approach also enables the synthesis of doped structures, such as Ag-doped MoS₂ (AMSx) nanoflowers, which exhibit enhanced sensitivity for bilirubin and Bisphenol A.

## 2.3 Phase Engineering

One of the most powerful strategies for optimizing SERS performance is phase engineering. TMDs like MoS₂ and WS₂ can exist in different structural phases: the semiconducting 2H phase and the more metallic 1T or 1T' phases. Phase-engineered 1T'-WS₂ and 1T'-MoS₂ monolayers have demonstrated SERS performances comparable to noble-metal substrates, significantly outperforming their 2H counterparts. This enhancement is linked to the high-purity distorted octahedral (1T') phase, which facilitates more efficient interfacial charge transfer.

## 2.4 Defect and Alloy Engineering

Controlling the density of defects, such as sulfur vacancies or high-valence metal atoms, provides a pathway to "tune" SERS activity. Defect-rich MoS₂ nanosheets exhibit metallic properties and enhanced interactions with target molecules, leading to significant Raman signal amplification. Furthermore, alloy engineering, such as the synthesis of few-layer manganese phosphorus trichalcogenides (Mn0.73Ni0.27PS₃), allows for "synergistic resonance" effects that combine efficient charge transfer with maximized excitation resonance.

## 3. SERS Enhancement Mechanism

The enhancement of Raman signals on 2D metal chalcogenide substrates is traditionally explained by two main mechanisms: the Electromagnetic Mechanism (EM) and the Chemical Mechanism (CM).

## 3.1 Electromagnetic Enhancement (EM)

EM is primarily attributed to the concentration of the local electric field by surface plasmons in the vicinity of nanostructured metal surfaces. While pristine 2D


semiconductors typically lack strong LSPR in the visible range, EM can be introduced through hybrid systems. Decoration of 2D nanosheets with noble metal nanoparticles (Ag, Au) creates dense and uniform "hotspots" that synergize with the chemical enhancement of the 2D substrate. For instance, combining MoS₂ with Ag nanowire-nanoparticle structures can yield enhancement factors up to 10⁶ .

## 3.2 Chemical Mechanism (CM) and Charge Transfer

The CM is the dominant enhancement mechanism for most 2D metal chalcogenides. It involves the modification of a molecule's Raman polarizability tensor upon adsorption. The primary component of CM is the charge transfer (CT) process between the substrate and the analyte.

Photoinduced Charge Transfer (PICT): Involves an electron leap between the molecule and the substrate during laser excitation. The probability of this leap is linearly related to the density of states (DOS) near the Fermi energy level.

Ground-State Charge Transfer (GSCT): Involves the redistribution of charge when the molecule and substrate form a complex in the electronic ground state.


## 3.3 Role of Density of States (DOS)

In metallic 2D materials like 2H-TaS₂ and NbS₂, the abundance of DOS near the Fermi level is critical. A high population of electrons near the Fermi level, often provided by continuous d-orbitals in transition metals, increases the probability of intermolecular charge transfer. Furthermore, the alignment between the substrate’s Fermi level and the molecule's highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) or lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) enhances the resonance effect, leading to ultrahigh sensitivities (EF up to 1.3 × 10¹⁴ for R6G on 2H-TaS₂).

## 3.4 Interface Dipoles and Fluorescence Quenching

Other factors contributing to signal enhancement include interface dipole interactions, which cause symmetry-related perturbations in the analyte. Additionally, many 2D chalcogenides, such as NbTe₂ and defect-rich MoS₂, exhibit strong fluorescence quenching effects, which "clean up" the Raman spectra by suppressing the background fluorescence of organic dyes.

## 4. Sensing Performance and Applications

2D metal chalcogenides have demonstrated exceptional performance in detecting a wide variety of chemical and biochemical analytes.

## 4.1 Environmental Pollutants and Dyes

Common organic dyes like Rhodamine 6G (R6G), Rhodamine B (RhB), and Crystal Violet (CV) are standard probes used to evaluate SERS activity. Substrates like 1T'-WS₂ and 1T'-MoS₂ achieve detection limits as low as 8.28 × 10⁻¹² M and 8.57 × 10⁻¹¹ M for R6G, respectively. Vertical WS₂ nanoflakes functionalized with Ag nanoparticles have pushed these limits even further, detecting RhB at 10⁻¹⁶ M and Methyl Orange (MO) at 10⁻¹⁷ M.

## 4.2 Antibiotics and Food Safety

Monitoring antibiotics in food and water is a critical application. Few-layer NbTe₂ nanosheets have been used for the ultrasensitive detection of ciprofloxacin (CIP) and enrofloxacin (ENR) with detection limits of 35.1 and 35.9 ppb, respectively. Ag-decorated WSe₂ has also been employed for highly sensitive nitrofurantoin detection.


In the realm of food analysis, metallic NbS₂ substrates have been utilized to distinguish between different types of red wines.

## 4.3 Biochemical and Disease Markers

For biomedical applications, these materials offer biocompatibility and stability in complex media. CVD-grown MoTe₂ films have demonstrated nanomolar detection of β-sitosterol, a potential disease marker, even within cell culture media. Ag-doped MoS₂ (AMSx) nanoflowers have shown high sensitivity for sensing bilirubin (a marker for liver function) and Bisphenol A with a detection limit of 10⁻⁹ M.

## 6. Future Perspectives and Conclusion

2D metal chalcogenides represent a transformative class of SERS substrates. Their primary advantage lies in the highly tunable chemical enhancement mechanism, which avoids the issues of spectral reproducibility and background noise associated with traditional plasmonic metals. The ability to manipulate their electronic structures through phase engineering, doping, and defect control allows for the rational design of substrates tailored to specific analytes.

However, several challenges remain. While metallic phases like 1T' offer superior performance, their long-term stability in ambient conditions can be lower than that of the 2H phase. Future research should focus on enhancing the environmental stability of these high-performance phases and expanding the library of detectable molecules to include more complex proteins and DNA sequences for clinical diagnostics.

In conclusion, 2D nanosheet materials of metal chalcogenides provide a versatile and sensitive platform for both chemical and biochemical sensing. Their unique density of states, efficient charge transfer capabilities, and compatibility with flexible electronic designs position them as leading candidates for the development of portable, high-sensitivity SERS-based analytical devices.
